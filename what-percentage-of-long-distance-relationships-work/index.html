What Percentage of Long-Distance Relationships Work?

There is no official success rate for long-distance relationships, but research points to about the same odds as couples who live close. In a nationally representative study of 870 people, long-distance partners were as likely to have broken up by follow-up as close couples. Among first-year college students in long-distance relationships, 57% were still with the same partner a semester later.

What percentage of long-distance relationships work?

The answer depends on what "work" means, because each study measures something different. Some follow couples for a few months, some ask whether the couple made it to the same city, and some ask people to rate their own relationship. The table shows what each measure found.

What "work" meansResultStudy

The college study gives the clearest number. Researchers followed first-year students at a large university in the northeastern U.S. Of the 194 students who started college in a long-distance relationship and answered again in spring, 110 were still with the same partner. Another 61 had broken up and were single, and 23 had broken up and found a new partner.

The reunion study allows a rough calculation. About half of the couples ended the relationship while apart. Two-thirds of the rest lasted the first three months together. So roughly one in three couples from the start made it through both stages. That is arithmetic from one sample, not a national rate.

Do long-distance relationships break up more than close ones?

No, not in the studies that tracked breakups. Most studies that compared relationship quality also found few differences.

  • Kelmer, Rhoades, Stanley and Markman (2013): In a nationally representative sample of 870 people, those in long-distance relationships reported higher relationship quality and more dedication, and felt less trapped. They also expected a lower chance of breaking up, yet broke up at the same rate as close couples by the follow-up.
  • Guldner and Swensen (1995): 194 people in long-distance relationships and 190 in close ones showed no significant differences in satisfaction, intimacy, trust or how the relationship progressed.
  • Dargie, Blair, Goldfinger and Pukall (2015): Among 1,142 participants, there were few differences between long-distance and close relationships. Individual and relationship traits predicted relationship quality better than the distance did.
  • Beckmeyer, Herbenick and Eastman-Mueller (2023): In the 2020 College Sexual Health Survey, happiness, commitment and conflict did not differ between students in long-distance and close relationships.
  • Stafford and Merolla (2007): Long-distance dating couples were more stable than close couples as long as they stayed apart.

The last study points to the catch. Long-distance couples tend to idealize each other more, and that picture gets tested once daily life starts.

When do long-distance relationships usually end?

Long-distance relationships tend to end at two points: during the separation and in the first months after moving together. Stafford, Merolla and Castle (2006) found that about half of long-distance dating couples moved to the same place, while the other half broke up while still apart. Among the couples who reunited, one-third ended the relationship within three months.

The people in that study described what changed after the move:

  • less personal freedom and time for themselves
  • learning more about the partner, both good and bad
  • trouble managing their time
  • more conflict and more jealousy

People whose relationships ended after the move were more likely to say they missed parts of the long-distance phase. A later study by Stafford and Merolla (2007) named two warning signs. Longer gaps between in-person visits and extreme idealization during the separation both predicted breakups after the couple moved together.

That gives you two practical levers before the move. Keep visits as regular as your budget and schedule allow, and let your partner see your ordinary days, not only the highlight reel of each reunion.

How many couples are in long-distance relationships?

No survey counts all long-distance dating couples in the U.S., so the best numbers come from colleges and from the Census data on married people. College data shows how common distance is early in adult life:

  • 34.2% of college students in a relationship at one university were in a long-distance one in the 2020 College Sexual Health Survey (Beckmeyer et al., 2023). It was more common among first-year students and less common among older students.
  • 30.4% of all first-year students at one northeastern university, singles included, were in a long-distance relationship in their first fall semester (Waterman et al., 2017). Among the students who had a partner at all, about four in five were long distance.
  • 36.5% of 647 young adults in a relationship were long distance in an online survey by Holtzman and colleagues (2021). The participants were students at one Canadian university.

The gap between these numbers comes from who was asked and when. A first-year student who left a partner at home is in a different situation from a senior dating someone on campus.

Long-distance marriage statistics: how many married couples live apart?

In 2024, about 7.6 million married people in the U.S. said their spouse lives somewhere else for reasons other than separation. That is 5.65% of married people who are not separated, or about 1 in 18. The figure comes from our analysis of the American Community Survey 2024 1-year estimates, table B12001, for people age 15 and over.

The Census Bureau calls this group "married, spouse absent, other." It includes people whose spouse works and lives away from home, serves away in the armed forces, or lives in an institution such as a care home. The survey does not record the reason for each person, so the number covers all of these situations together.

Two details matter when you quote this number:

  • It counts people, not couples. 4.05 million men and 3.55 million women are in this group. Halving the total does not give the number of couples. Some spouses live abroad and are not in the survey, while in other couples both partners are counted.
  • It is not a count of unhappy marriages. Spouses who live apart because of marriage problems count as "separated" and are not part of the 7.6 million.

Which long-distance relationship statistics are wrong?

Several of the most shared numbers about long-distance relationships are misquoted or have no traceable source. Here is where each one comes from.

Popular claimWhere it comes fromWhat holds up
"14 million couples are in long-distance relationships"Center for the Study of Long Distance RelationshipsThe center's own estimate was just over 7 million couples, or 14 to 15 million individuals, built on 2005 data. The total matches 3.57 million married people plus 3.5 million dating couples, so even the 7 million mixes people and couples.
"3.5 million married couples live apart"Same center, repeated by blogsThe center reported 3.57 million married people, not couples, for 2005. The 2024 Census figure is 7.6 million married people. The two counts may use different surveys, so they are not a trend.
"40% of long-distance relationships break up, after 4.5 months on average"Repeated widely without a citationNo study, sample or year is attached to it. Studies that followed couples found breakup rates close to those of close couples.
"58% of long-distance relationships succeed"2018 OnePoll survey commissioned by KIIROO, a maker of interactive sex toys1,000 adults with long-distance experience rated their own relationship. "Success" was not defined.
"One-third of couples break up after closing the distance"Stafford, Merolla & Castle, 2006Real, but it applies only to couples who moved together, within three months. Some sites credit it to a different paper from 2007.
"70% of couples who skip ground rules break up"The center's website, citing its own studyNo published paper is named. Treat it as an observation, not a statistic.

What makes a long-distance relationship more likely to last?

The research points to a few habits more than to the distance itself. These findings come from surveys, so they show links, not proof of cause.

  1. The relationship itself matters more than the miles. Dargie and colleagues (2015) found that individual and relationship traits predicted relationship quality, while long-distance status made little difference.
  2. Text often, and answer with care. Holtzman and colleagues (2021) found that frequent, responsive texting went along with higher satisfaction in long-distance relationships, but not in close ones. Video calls were not linked to satisfaction in either group.
  3. Share more than updates. In a diary study, Jiang and Hancock (2013) found that long-distance couples opened up more and felt more intimate in their conversations than close couples.
  4. Keep the gaps between visits short and the picture real. Stafford and Merolla (2007) linked long stretches without a visit and extreme idealization to breakups after the move.
  5. Talk about the move before it happens. Freedom, time management and conflict were the issues couples named after reuniting, so they make a good agenda for that conversation.

Frequently asked questions

What percentage of long-distance relationships work?

There is no national success rate. In the studies that followed couples over time, long-distance relationships broke up at about the same rate as couples who live close. In one study of first-year college students, 57% of those in long-distance relationships were still with the same partner a semester later.

Do long-distance relationships last longer than close ones?

While the partners still live apart, they can. Stafford and Merolla (2007) found long-distance dating couples more stable than close couples during the separation. The risk rose once they moved to the same place: in an earlier study, one in three reunited couples broke up within three months.

How many married couples live apart in the US?

In the 2024 American Community Survey, about 7.6 million married people said their spouse lives somewhere else for reasons other than separation, such as work, military service or a stay in an institution. That is about 5.7% of married people who are not separated. The Census counts people, not couples.

Is it true that 40% of long-distance relationships fail?

The claim that 40% of long-distance relationships end, after 4.5 months on average, circulates without a named study, sample or year. Studies that did follow couples found breakup rates close to those of couples who live near each other.

What is the 777 rule for long-distance relationships?

The 7-7-7 rule is a popular rule of thumb for couples: a date every 7 days, a weekend away every 7 weeks and a vacation every 7 months. It is not based on research. For long-distance couples, the closest finding is that longer gaps between in-person visits predicted more trouble after the couple moved together.
